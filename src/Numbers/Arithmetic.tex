\section{Divisor}

Let \(a,b \in \Z\). We say that \(a\) \emph{divides} \(b\) if 

\[
	a \mid b \iff \exists k \in \Z : b = ak.
\]

\section{Even and Odd Numbers}

A natural number \(a\) is \emph{even} if \(a = 2k\) with \(k \in \N\).

A natural number \(b\) is \emph{odd} if \(b = 2q - 1\) with \(q \in \N\).

\section{Perfect Squares}

A natural number \(a\) is a \emph{perfect square} if \(\sqrt{a} \in \N\).

\section{General Divisibility Rules}

For \(x, y, z, a, b \in \Z\) are the follwoing statements true: 

\begin{itemize}

	\item \(x \mid y\) and \(y \mid z\) follows that \(x \mid z\).

	\item \(0 \mid x \implies x = 0\).

	\item \(x \mid 0\) for all \(x\).

	\item \(\pm \mid x\) for all \(x\).

	\item \(x \mid \pm 1 \implies x = \pm 1\)

	\item \(x \mid y\) and \(y \mid x\) implies \(x = \pm y\).

	\item \(x\ mid y\) and \(x \mid z\) implies \(x \mid ay + b z\).

\end{itemize}

\section{General Division Rules}

\subsection*{Sum Rules}

If the number \(a\) can be expressed as a sum and \(b\) is a common divisor of all the terms, 
then \(b\) is also a divisor of \(a\).

\textbf{Example:} 

\( 742 = 700 + 42 \). Then 7, as a common divisor of 700 and 42, is a divisor of 742.

\subsection*{Product Rule}

If the number \(a\) can be written as a product of two factors and \(b\) is a divisor of one of these factors, then \(b\) is also a divisor of \(a\).

\textbf{Example:}

\( 1500 = 15 \cdot 100 \). Then 3 and 5 are divisors of 15, and 2, 4, 5, 10, 25, and 50 are divisors of 
100—thus, all are divisors of 1500.

\subsubsection*{Divisor Pair Rules}

If the number \(a\) has \(b\) as a divisor, then \( \frac{a}{b} \) is also a divisor of \(a\).

\textbf{Example:}

3 is a divisor of 315. Then \( 105 = \frac{315}{3} \) is also a divisor of 315.

\subsection*{Divisor Rule}

If the number \(a\) has \(b\) as a divisor, then every divisor of \(b\) is also a divisor of 
\(a\).

\textbf{Example:}

45 is a divisor of 450. Then 3, 5, 9, and 15 (divisors of 45) are also divisors of 450.

\subsection*{Difference Rule}

If the number \(a\) can be expressed as a difference and \(b\) is a common divisor of the minuend 
and subtrahend, then \(b\) is also a divisor of \(a\).

\textbf{Example:}

\( 686 = 700 - 14 \). Then 7, as a common divisor of 700 and 14, is a divisor of 686.

\subsection*{Relative Prime Rule}

If the number \(a\) has two relatively prime numbers \(b\) and \( c \) as divisors, then their 
product \( b \cdot c \) is also a divisor of \(a\).

\textbf{Example:}

1540 has the relatively prime numbers 7 and 11 as divisors. Then \( 77 = 7 \cdot 11 \) is also a divisor 
of 1540.

\section{Using Modular Arithmetic to Find the n-th Digit of a Large Number}

What is the last digit of the number \(13^{2023}\)?

Note that \(13 \mod 10 = 3\) and  \(13^2 \mod 10 =  ((13 \mod 10)  (13\mod 10)) \mod 10 = 9 \).

We can continue this process, and we will notice a pattern. After some iterations, we will know that

\[
    (13^{2020} 13) \mod 10 = 7.
\]

\section{Gaussian Brackets}

The Gaussian brackets refer to two closely related functions used to round real numbers to integers in specific ways:

\subsection{Floor Function}

The floor function, denoted by \(\lfloor x \rfloor\), gives the greatest integer less than or equal 
to a real number \(x\).

\textbf{Example:}

\(\lfloor 3.7 \rfloor = 3\), \(\lfloor -1.2 \rfloor = -2\)

\subsection{Ceiling Function} 
    
The ceiling function, denoted by \(\lceil x \rceil\), gives the smallest integer greater than or 
equal to a real number \(x\).

\textbf{Example:}

\(\lceil 3.7 \rceil = 4\), \(\lceil -1.2 \rceil = -1\)

These functions are particularly useful in number theory, computer science, and rounding operations, where precise integer bounds of real numbers are required.

\section{Residual Class Ring Modulo \texorpdfstring{\(n\)}{}}

Given \(n \in \N\), we define the relation \(\sim_n\) for \(\Z\).

\[
    a \sim b \iff n \mid (a - b).
\]

Also, \(a / n\) and \(b / n\) have the same remainder.

\subsection{Equivalence Class of \texorpdfstring{\(\Z\)}{}} 

For \(i, n \in \Z\) 

\[
    \overline{i} := i + n \Z = \{ i + na \mid a \in \Z\}
\]

and 

\[
    \Z_N = \Z / n \Z = \{\overline{0}:= n \Z, \overline{1}:= 1 + n \Z, \dots, \overline{n - 1} := n - 1 + n \Z \},
\]

is the set of all equivalence classes for \(\Z\) with respect to the relation 
\(k \sim l \iff n \mid (k - l)\). We define the addition and multiplication for \(\Z / n \Z\) using the representatives:

\[
    (a  + n \Z) + (b + n \Z) := (a + b) + n\Z
\]

and 

\[
    (a  + n \Z) \cdot (b + n \Z) := ab + n\Z
\]

For all \(a, b \in \Z\). Therefore, 

\[
    \overline{a} + \overline{b} = \overline{a + b}, \quad \overline{a} \cdot \overline{b} = \overline{ab}.
\]

\section{Minimal Multiple}

For all integers \(a \in \Z_{\ge 0}\). 0 is the only multiple of \(a\) between \(a\) and 0.

\textbf{Proof:}

Let us suppose there exist an integer \(z\) with \(0 < z < a\) such that \(a \mid z\). Then 

\[
	z = aq
\]

Since \(a > z\), if \(q > 0\), then \(aq > qz > z\), for \(q < 0\) we have that \(aq < qz < z\) and finally 
for \(q = 0\) we get that \(z = 0\), thus we have a contradiction.

\QED

\section{Division Theorem}

Given any two integers \(a\) and \(b\) with \(a > 0\), there exist unique integers \(q\) and \(r\) such that 
\(a = qb + r\) with \(0 \ge r < b\).

This is very important as it allows to write a number in terms of another with ease by just using our number a the left side and the other on the right side with 
the product and the residual \(\text{target}\, = q(\text{some number}) + r\). Of course, obeying the constraints that our target is greater or equal that our current number.

\textbf{Proof:}

We first will prove that such integers exists. We do this by first reduce our scope to \(b > 0\) and prove the 
existence of \(r\) by constructing the following set. 

\[
	S = \{a - bq \mid q \in \Z, \, a - bx \ge 0\}
\]

Note that \(S\) is not empty since, for \(a \ge 0\) we can choose \(q = 0\) giving us 

\[
	a - 0 = a \in S
\]

and for \(a < 0\) we can choose \(q = a\) giving us since \(b > 0\)

\[ 
	a_{< 0}(1 - b)_{< 0} \ge 0.
\] 

Since \(S\) is not empty at it only contains positive integers we can apply the \emph{well-ordering-principle} which tells us that 
every subset of the Natural has minimum which we will call \(r\). Hence \(r = a - bq \ge 0\). We now need to 
show that \(0 \le r < b\), by contradiction.

If \(r \ge b\), then 

\begin{align*}
	a - qb &\ge b \\	
	a - qb - b &= a - (q + 1)b \ge 0
\end{align*}

Hence, \(r - b \in S\), but \(r - b < r\) and thus we have a contradiction since \(r\) is the minimum of \(S\).

For the uniqueness, we suppose that \(q, q'\) and \(r, r'\) satisfy our equality and we also assume without loosing generality that \(r' \ge r\).

Then from our equality we can get the following

\[
	b(q - q') = r' - r
\]

where \(0 \ge r' - r < b\) and the left-hand-side is a multiple of \(b\). Now now note, the only multiple of \(b\) which is between 
\(b\) and zero without including \(b\) itself is \(0\) hence \(r = r'\) and because of the equality \(q = q'\).

For \(b < 0\) we use the case of \(-b > 0\) and also use \(-q\) and do the same.

\QED

\section{Greatest Common Divisor}

For two integers \(a, b \in \Z\) with \((a,b) \neq (0,0)\), the positive integer \(t\) satisfying:

\begin{itemize}

	\item \(t \mid a\) and \(t \mid b\).

	\item For all \(c \in \Z\) with \(c \mid a\) and \(c \mid b\), it follows that \(c \mid t\).

\end{itemize}

We call it the \emph{greatest common divisor} (gcd) of \(a\) and \(b\), denoted as \(t = \gcd(a,b)\).

If \(t = 1\), then \(a\) and \(b\) are said to be \emph{coprime} or \emph{relatively prime}.

\section{The Euclidean Algorithm}

If \(a = bq + r\) then \(\gcd(a, b) = \gcd(b, r)\). 

\textbf{Input:} \(a, b \in \Z\), \(a \neq 0 \neq b\).  

\textbf{Output:} The greatest common divisor \(t\) of \(a\) and \(b\).

\textbf{Algorithm:}

\begin{enumerate}

	\item If \(a = 0\), then return \(|b|\). If \(b = 0\), return \(|a|\).

	\item Set \(r_1 := a\); \(r_2 := b\); \(k := 2\).

	\item As long as \(r_k \neq 0\), define \(r_{k+1} := r_{k - 1} \mod r_k\) or equivalently and easier 
	\[
		r_{k + 1} = r_k - q r_{k - 1}, \quad q \in \Z
	\]

	\item As soon as \(r_k = 0\), return \(r_{k-1}\).

\end{enumerate}

In simpler terms, as long as the remainder is not zero, we keep dividing the previous remainder by the current
remainder and updating the remainders. When we reach a remainder of zero, the previous remainder is the gcd. 

\textbf{Proof:}

Notice that the algorithm terminates because the sequence given \(r_n\) is monotone decreasing due to \(0 \le r < b\) and 
the well-ordering principle of the natural numbers.

For the correctness: given is \(a = bq + r\) to show that \(\gcd(a, b) = \gcd(b, r)\). This is like saying:

\begin{align*}
	\gcd(a,b) & = \gcd(b, r) \\ 
	\gcd(b,r) & = \gcd(r, r_1)\\
	\gcd(r, r_1) & = \gcd(r_1, r_2) \\
	& \vdots \\
	\gcd(r_{n-1}, r_{n}) & = \gcd(r_n, 0) = r_n.
\end{align*}

Let \(d\) be a common divisor of \(a\), \(b\). Then \(d \mid a\) and \(d \mid b\), so \(d \mid ((a - bq) = r) \implies d \mid r \).

Let \(e\) be a common divisor of \(b\), \(r\). Then \(e \mid b\) and \(e \mid r\), so \(e \mid ((bq + r) = a) \implies e \mid a \).

Thus, the set of common divisors of \(a\), \(b\) is the same as the set of common divisors of \(b\), \(r\),
so their greatest common divisors are equal.

\QED 

\textbf{Example:}

Find \(\gcd(252, 198)\):

\begin{align*}
	252 & = 1 \cdot 198 + 54  \\
	198 & = 3 \cdot 54 + 36   \\
	54  & = 1 \cdot 36 + 18   \\
	36  & = 2 \cdot 18 + 0
\end{align*}

So, \(\gcd(252, 198) = 18\). 

\subsection{Euclidean Algorithm as Linear Map}

Given \(E := \{(a,b)^T \mid a, b \in \Z, b \ne 0 \}\) and 

\[
	\varepsilon: E \to \Z^{2 \times 1} : 
	\begin{bmatrix}
		a \\ 
		b
	\end{bmatrix}
	\mapsto
	\begin{bmatrix}
		b \\
		a \mod b
	\end{bmatrix}
\]

Then the Euclidean algorithm can be described as repeated application of \(\varepsilon\) until the second
component is zero. The first component at that point is the gcd.

\[
	\varepsilon^{k - 1}
	\begin{bmatrix}
		a \\ 
		b
	\end{bmatrix}
	= \varepsilon \circ \cdots \circ \varepsilon
	\begin{bmatrix}
		t \\ 
		0
	\end{bmatrix}
\]

\(t = \gcd(a,b)\).

Also, we can express \(\varepsilon\) as a matrix multiplication:

\[
	\varepsilon
	\begin{bmatrix}
		a \\ 
		b
	\end{bmatrix}
	=
	\begin{bmatrix}
		0 & 1 \\
		1 & -q
	\end{bmatrix}
	\begin{bmatrix}
		a \\ 
		b
	\end{bmatrix}
	= 
	\begin{bmatrix}
		b \\ 
		r
	\end{bmatrix}
\]

with \(a = qb + r\) and \(q \in \Z, 0 \le q < |b|\).

\section{Bezout Identity}

This is one of the most important properties of the \emph{gcd}. 
Given \(a, b \in \Z\), not both zero, there exist integers \(x, y \in \Z\) such that

\[
	\gcd(a, b) = ax + by
\]

This equation comes directly from \emph{Euclid's Algorithm}. For the intuition, when writing your iterations 
you can manipulate them to arrive at the solution by substituting the previous reminders in to its form of \(r_{q} - kr_{k}\).

\textbf{Proof:}

To prove this we will use a construction of a set

\[
	M := \{ax + by \mid ax + b y > 0 \land x \in \Z \land y \in \Z\}
\]

We will prove that \(\min(M) = \gcd(a, b)\).


We know that \(M\) is not empty because \(\alpha, \beta \in M\), and it has a minimum by the well-ordering principle 
of the natural numbers which we will call \(d = a x_0 + b y_0\). Now we will use that fact any integer can be written as a product plus 
reminder by writing \(a\) as 

\[
	a = qd + r
\]

Now we proceed to 

\begin{align*}	
	a &= qd + r \\
	r &= a - qd \\
	&= a - q( a x_0 + b y_0)\\
	&= a - qax_0 - bqy_0\\
	&= a(1 - qx_0) + b (-qy_0)
\end{align*}

This is also a member of \(M\) since \(1 - qx_0\) and \(-qy_0\) are also integers. Also note that 

\[
	0 < a - qd < ax_0 + by_0
\]

But this would be a contradiction to the fact that \(d\) is the minimum of \(M\). Therefore, \(r = 0\) thus, 
giving us \(d \mid a\). Using the same argument with \(q'\) and \(r'\) we would arrive at the same conclusion for \(b\). 
Now we have a common divisor, leaving us with only proving that it is in fact the greatest.

Let \(c\) be a common divisor of both \(a\) and \(b\). Then, \(c \mid ax_0 + by_0\). Note that any common divisor of \(a\) and \(b\)
also divides \(d\), hence it is the greatest common divisor.

\QED

\textbf{Example}

Find \(\gcd(252, 198)\) and the Bezout coefficients:

\begin{align*}
	252 & = 1 \cdot 198 + 54  \\
	198 & = 3 \cdot 54 + 36   \\
	54  & = 1 \cdot 36 + 18   \\
	36  & = 2 \cdot 18 + 0
\end{align*}

So, \(\gcd(252, 198) = 18\). Now we work backwards 


\begin{align*}	
	18 &= 54 - 36 \\
	   &= 54 - (198 - 3(54)) \\
	   &= -198 + 4(54)) \\
\end{align*}

So, \(\alpha = 4\) and \(\beta = -1\).

\section{Prime Field}

Given \(p \in \N, p > 1\) is prime, then \(\Z / p \Z\) is a field with \(p\) elements. It can also 
be noted \(\field_p\).

\textbf{Proof:} 

We need to show that for \(a + p \Z \ne p \Z\) there exists some \(b + p \Z\) such that \((a + p\Z))(b + p\Z) = 1 + p\Z\).

For \(a \notin p \Z\) we know that \(gcd(a,p) = 1\), thus we do not have zero divisors.

\subsection{Implication of Prime Numbers}

For \(a, b \in \Z\) and a prime number \(p\)

\[
	p \mid ab \implies (p \mid a ) \lor (p \mid b).
\]

\textbf{Proof:} 

``\(\Rightarrow\)'': Let \(p\) be a prime and \(a,b \in \Z\) with \(p \mid ab\). Assume \(p \nmid a\). Then there exist \(\alpha,\beta \in \Z\)
with \(\alpha p + \beta a = 1\). Since \(p \mid ab\), it follows that \(p \mid \alpha p b + \beta ab = (\alpha p + \beta a)b = b\).

``\(\Leftarrow\)'': Let \(c \in \N\) be a divisor of \(p\). Then there exists \(d \in \N\) with \(p = cd\). In particular \(1 \le c \le p\) and
\(1 \le d \le p\) and \(p \mid cd\). By our assumption it follows that \(p \mid c\) or \(p \mid d\), i.e. \(p \le c\) or \(p \le d\). Together with
\(c \le p\) and \(d \le p\) this forces \(c = 1\) or \(c = p\). Hence, \(p\) has no nontrivial divisors and is prime.

\QED

\section{Fundamental Theorem of Arithmetic}

Every integer greater than 1 can be written in the form

\[
	p_1^{n_1}p_2^{n_2} \cdots p_k^{n_k},
\]

where \(n_i \geq 0\) and the \(p_i\) are distinct primes. The factorization is unique, except possibly for 
the order of the factors.

\textbf{Example:}

\[
	4312 = 2 \cdot 2156 = 2 \cdot 2 \cdot 1078 = 2 \cdot 2 \cdot 2 \cdot 539 = 2 \cdot 2 \cdot 2 \cdot 7 
	\cdot 77 = 2 \cdot 2 \cdot 2 \cdot 7 \cdot 7 \cdot 11
\]

That is,

\[
	4312 = 2^3 \cdot 7^2 \cdot 11
\]

\subsection{Least Common Multiple}

The least common multiple of \(a\) and \(b\), denoted \([a, b]\), is the smallest positive integer that is 
a multiple of both \(a\) and \(b\).

\textbf{Example:}

\[
	[6, 4] = 12, \quad [33, 15] = 165
\]

\subsection{Multiplication Property of the Least Common Multiple}

\[
	[a, b] \cdot \gcd(a, b) = ab
\]

\textbf{Proof:}

Let

\[
	a = p_1 \cdots p_lq_1 \cdots q_m, \quad b = q_1 \cdots q_mr_1 \cdots r_n,
\]

then

\begin{align*}
	\gcd(a, b) & = q_1 \cdots q_m                                 \\
	[a, b]     & = p_1 \cdots p_lq_1 \cdots q_mr_1 \cdots r_n     \\
	ab         & = p_1 \cdots p_lq_1^2 \cdots q_m^2r_1 \cdots r_n.
\end{align*}

So

\[
	[a, b] \cdot \gcd(a, b) = ab.
\]

\textbf{Example:}

\[
	\gcd(36, 90) = 18, \quad [36, 90] = 180, \quad 36 \cdot 90 = 32400 = 18 \cdot 180
\]

\section{Prime Factorization}

To find the \emph{prime factorization} of a number \(a\), the algorithm goes as follows: 

\begin{itemize}

	\item Let \(n\) be the first prime number. 

	\item  Try dividing \(a\) by \(n\). If \(n \mid a\) note the number and set \(a = a \div n\) to repeat this step. If \(n\) 
	did not divide \(a\) set \(n\) to the next prime number and repeat this step. 

	\item When 1 was reached using the previous step then the algorithm terminates and the noted numbers are our prime factorization 
	of \(a\).

\end{itemize}

\section{GCD via Prime Factorization}

The \(\gcd\) of two or more numbers can also be found with the following algorithm.

\begin{itemize}
	
	\item Find the prime factorization of all target numbers.

	\item Note the numbers which appear in both prime factorizations, always taking the one with the least exponent.
	
	\item Multiply the noted prime factors together. 
	
\end{itemize}

\section{Modular Arithmetic}

We define an equivalence relation 

\[
    a \equiv b \mod n \iff n | (a - b) \land  a,b \in \Z.
\]

or equivalently 

\[
    a \equiv b \mod n \iff  a - b = nk\, \land  a,b,k \in \Z.
\]

This means that both have the \textbf{same remainder} when divided by \(n\).

\subsection{Properties of Modular Arithmetic}

\begin{itemize}
    
    \item  \emph{Addition}:
    \[
        (a + b) \mod n = (a \mod n + b \mod n) \mod n
    \]

    \item \emph{multiplication}:
    \[
        (a \cdot b) \mod n = (a \mod n \cdot b \mod n) \mod n
    \]

    \item \emph{Potenziation}:
    \[
        (a^m) \mod n = ({(a \mod n)}^m) \mod n
    \]

    \item \emph{Division}:
    \[
        \left(\frac{a}{b}\right) \mod n \ne  \frac{a \mod n}{b \mod n}
    \]

\end{itemize}


\section{Residual Classes and Residual Class Rings}

Because we have an equivalence relation, we can classify the partitions of our set \(\Z\) into classes, which we will call \emph{residual classes}.

\textbf{Example:}

\[
    [13]_7 + \{m \in \Z \mid m \equiv 13 \mod 7\}
\]

The set of all residual classes can be written as \(\Z / n \Z\), where \(n\) is the modulus. 

\subsection{Addition and Multiplication}

For the residual class rigns we can define 

\[
	[a] + [b] = [a + b]
\]

and 

\[
	[a] \cdot [b] = [a \cdot b]
\]

which are both well defined functions.

\textbf{Proof:}

For \(a \in [a]\) and \(b \in [b]\) we have that 

\[
	qp + r_1 + kp + r_2 = p(q + k) + r_1 + r_2
\]

which is member of a class on its onw \([a + b]\). Similar for the multiplication. 

\QED
