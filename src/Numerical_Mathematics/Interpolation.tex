\chapter{Polynomial Interpolation}

\emph{Polynomial interpolation} is a family of procedures to find a polynomial given 
a certain numbers of data points \((x_0, y_0), \dots, (x_n, y_n)\) such that 

\[
    f(x_i) = y_i, \quad \forall i = 0, 1, \dots, n.
\]

This results into finding a polynomial with at most degree\(n\), with \(n + 1\) terms and \(n\) constant terms \(c_i\)

\[
    f(x) = c_0 + c_1 x + c_2 x^2 + \dots + c_{n}x^n
\]

\section{Set of Polynomials of Degree \texorpdfstring{\(n\)}{n}}

We define the set of polynomials with at degree \(n\) as \(P_n\).

\subsection{Maximal Number of Zeros For A Polynomial}

A polynomial \(p \in P_n\) has at most \(n\) zeros or is identical 0.

\subsection{Uniqueness of The Polynomial Interpolation}

Given a set of points \((x_0, y_0), \dots, (x_n, y_n)\) there exists only one polynomial of degree at most \(n\) 
such that \(f(x_0) = y_0, \dots\).

\textbf{Proof:}

\emph{Uniqueness}: Let us assume that such a polynomial \(p(x)\) exists, and also \(q(x) \ne p(x)\) such that both interpolate our 
points.

Using the fact that a polynomial \(P_n\) has at most \(n\) zeros or is identical 0.

If we perform the following operation

\[
    p(x) - q(x) = 0, \quad \forall x \in \{x \mid (x, y), \ y = 0\}
\]

We have a polynomial \(p(x) - q(x) \in P_n\) but it has zeros at \(n + 1\) places, hence \(p(x) - q(x) = 0 \implies p(x) = q(x)\).

\section{Vandermonde Systems}

For our interpolation \(p(x)\) we define the following system 

\[
    Vc 
    =
    \begin{bmatrix}
        1 & x_0 & \dots & x_0^n \\ 
        \vdots & \vdots &  & \vdots \\ 
        1 & x_n & \dots & x_n^n \\ 
    \end{bmatrix}
    \begin{bmatrix}
        c_0 \\ 
        \vdots \\ 
        c_n
    \end{bmatrix}
    = 
    \begin{bmatrix}
        y_0 \\ 
        \vdots \\ 
        y_n
    \end{bmatrix}
    = y
\]

\(Vc = y\) as our \emph{Vandermonde system} with \(V\) as the \emph{Vandermonde matrix}.

Now our problem of finding the constants in the vector \(c\) has been transformed into finding the inverse of \(V\).

\subsection{Invertibility Of The Vandermonde Matrix}

There exits an \(V^{-1}\) to the corresponding \(V\).

\textbf{Proof:}

\(\exists V^{-1} \iff \det(V) \ne 0\), therefore we need to compute the determinant in the following way.

Remember that row/column operations do not change the determinant 

\[
    \det(A) = \det(A')  =\det(C_i A) = \det(C_i)\det(A) = 1 \cdot \det(A)
\]

For 

\[
    V = 
    \begin{bmatrix}
        1 & x_0 & x_0^2 & \dots & x_0^n \\ 
        1 & x_1 & x_1^2 & \dots & x_1^n \\ 
        \vdots &  \vdots & \vdots&  & \vdots \\ 
        1 & x_n & x_n^2 & \dots & x_n^n \\ 
    \end{bmatrix}
\]

We add from the first column times \(-x_0\), then the second \(x_0 x_1\) and so on for each column

\begin{align*}
    \det(V) &= \det
    \begin{bmatrix}
        1 & x_0 & x_0^2 & \dots & x_0^n \\ 
        1 & x_1 & x_1^2 & \dots & x_1^n \\ 
        \vdots &  \vdots & \vdots&  & \vdots \\ 
        1 & x_n & x_n^2 & \dots & x_n^n
    \end{bmatrix} \\ 
    &= 
    \det
    \begin{bmatrix}
        1 & 0 & 0 & \dots & 0 \\ 
        1 & x_1 & x_1^2 & \dots & x_1^n \\ 
        \vdots &  \vdots & \vdots&  & \vdots \\ 
        1 & x_n & x_n^2 & \dots & x_n^n
    \end{bmatrix} \\ 
    &= 
    \det
    \begin{bmatrix}
        1 & 0 & 0 & \dots & 0 \\ 
        1 & x_1 - x_0 & x_1^2 -x_0 x_1 & \dots & x_1^n  - x_0x_1^{n - 1}\\ 
        \vdots &  \vdots & \vdots&  & \vdots \\ 
        1 & x_n - x_0 & x_n^2 -x_0 x_n & \dots & x_n^n - x_0 x_n^{n - 1}
    \end{bmatrix} \\ 
    &= 
    \det
    \begin{bmatrix}
        1 & 0 & 0 & \dots & 0 \\ 
        1 & x_1 - x_0 & x_1(x_1^2 -x_0) & \dots & x_1^{n - 1}( x_1 - x_0)\\ 
        \vdots &  \vdots & \vdots&  & \vdots \\ 
        1 & x_n - x_0 &x_n (x_n - x_0) & \dots & x_n^{n - 1}(x_n - x_0)
    \end{bmatrix} \\ 
    &= (x_1 - x_0)(x_2 - x_0) \cdot \dots \cdot (x_n - x_0) \det
    \begin{bmatrix}
        1 & x_1 & \dots & x_1^{n - 1} \\ 
        \vdots & \vdots &  & \vdots \\ 
        1 & x_n & \dots & x_n^{n - 1} 
    \end{bmatrix}
\end{align*}

By repeatedly applying the column subtraction, we get a transformed matrix from which we can extract the scalar factors from each 
column and then using the Laplace method for determinants, repeat the process for the inner sub-matrix.

Giving us an inductive procedure and the following results 

\[
    \det(V) = \prod_{0 \le i < j \le n} (x_j - x_i) \neq 0 \iff x_i \ne x_j \ \forall i \ne j.
\]

\section{Lagrange Polynomials}

\emph{Lagrange polynomial} are used to interpolate polynomial by using the following property

\[
    L_j(x_i) = 
    \begin{cases}
       1, & \, i = j \\  
       0, &  
    \end{cases}
\]

This is done by the definition 

\[
    L_j (x) = \prod_{i = 0}^{j - 1} \frac{x - x_i}{x_j- x_i} \prod_{i = 1}^{n} \frac{x - x_{j + i}}{x_j - x_{j + i}} = \prod_{i = 0, i \ne j}^{n} \frac{x - x_i}{x_j - x_i}
\]

This comes from the fact that given our points \(x_0, x_1, \dots x_n\) and we want for \(x_n\) that \(L_m(x_m) = 1\) and zero else where we get 

\[
    (x - x_1)(x - x_2)\cdot \dots \cdot (x - x_{m - 1})(x - x_{m + 1}) \cdot \dots \cdot \dot (x - x_n) \ne 1, \quad \text{but zero for every other}\, x_k
\]

To get it to be 1, we just need to normalize the term

\[
    \frac{(x - x_1)(x - x_2)\cdot \dots \cdot (x - x_{m - 1})(x - x_{m + 1}) \cdot \dots \cdot \dot (x - x_n)}{(x_m - x_1)(x_m - x_2)\cdot \dots \cdot (x_m - x_{m - 1})(x_m - x_{m + 1}) \cdot \dots \cdot \dot (x_m - x_n)}
\]

Our Lagrange polynomial is defined as 

\[
    p(x) = \sum_{i = 0}^{n} y_i L_i(x_i) = \sum_{i = 0}^{n} y_i  \left[\prod_{i = 0, i \ne j}^{n} \frac{x - x_i}{x_j - x_i}\right]
\]

\textbf{Example:}

Given are the points \((0, 3), (1, 2), (3, 6)\) find the interpolating polynomial.

\[
    L_0(x) = \frac{(x - 1)(x - 3)}{(0 - 1)(0 - 3)} = \frac{(x - 1)(x - 3)}{3} 
\]

\[
    L_1(x) = \frac{(x - 0)(x - 3)}{(1 - 0)(1 - 3)} = \frac{(x)(x - 3)}{-2}
\]

\[
    L_2(x) = \frac{(x - 0)(x - 1)}{(3 - 0)(3 - 1)} = \frac{(x)(x - 1)}{6}
\]

Resulting in the following interpolating polynomial

\[
    p(x) = 3 \cdot \frac{(x - 1)(x - 3)}{3} + 2 \cdot \frac{(x)(x - 3)}{-2} + 6 \cdot \frac{(x)(x - 1)}{6} = x^2 - x + 3
\]


\section{Newton Polynomials}

The interpolation polynomial in the \emph{Newtonian form} is a defined as 

\[
    p(x) = y_0 + a_1(x - x_0) + a_2(x - x_0)(x - x_1) + \dots + a_n \prod_{i = 0}^{n-1} (x - x_i)
\]

Note, that for for \(p(x) = y_0 + a_1(x - x_0)\) if we put \(p(x_0) = y0\) which is a quality we want.  If we set 
our polynomial to \(y_1\) we also get the first \emph{divided difference} 

\[
    y_1 = y_0 + a_1 (x_1 - x_0) \iff a_1 = \frac{y_1 - y_0}{x_1 - x_0} = f[x_0, x_1]
\]

where \(f[x_i, x_j]\) is the \emph{divided difference} defined as

\[
    f[x_i, x_j] = \frac{f[x_i, j -1] - f[x_{i - 1}, x_{j - 1}]}{x_i - x_{i - j}}
\]

with 

\[
    f[x_i, 0] = y_i
\]


We can keep the step we did without a recurrence relation for the next higher degree polynomials and get all of our differences.

\section{Polynomial Spline Interpolation}

For \(x_0 < x_1 < \dots < x_n\) we denote \(\Omega_n = \{x_0, \dots, x_n\}\) the \emph{subdivision of the interval} \([x_0, x_n]\). A function 
\(s_k\) such that 

\[
    s_k \mid_{[x_i, x_{i + 1}]} \text{ is a polynomial of degree } c \le k,
\]

\[
    s_k \text{ is } C^{k - 1} \text{ for } [x_0, x_n],
\]

where \(C^{k - 1}\) means differentiable to degree at most \(k - 1\); is denoted as \emph{polynomial spline} of order \(k\) with respect to \(\Omega_n\).

\emph{Polynomial splines} are a way of interpolating a polynomial to fit a continuous function in certain interval, 
and then combining those piecewise function into one continuous functions. This can be used when an interpolation method delivers 
errors certain regions of the domain. To address the discontinuities we use constrains using the derivatives of our polynomials.

\subsection{Cubic Splines}

A \emph{cubic polynomial spline} of degree 3 has the form 

\[
    s_3 \mid_{[x_i, x_{i + 1}]}(x_i) =  a_i + b_i (x  - x_i) + c_i (x  - x_i)^2 +d_i (x  - x_i)^3.
\]

Note that \(s_3\) is two times differentiable. 

We get our main conditions from 

\begin{align*}
    s_3(x_1) &= y_i \\ 
    &\vdots \\ 
    s_3(x_i) &= y_i
\end{align*}

and our smoothness conditions from 

\begin{align*}
    C^0: s_3(x_j)_- &= s_3(x_j)_+ \\ 
    C^1: s_3'(x_j)_- &= s_3'(x_j)_+ \\ 
    C^2: s_3''(x_i)_- &= s_3''(x_j)_+ 
\end{align*}

They just mean that our function will be continuous on the points where two splines meet.

Other types of conditions are for example: 

the \emph{natural spline}

\[
    s_3''(x_0) = s_3 '' (x_n) = 0,
\]

the \emph{periodic spline}

\[
    s_3'(x_0) = s_3'(x_n) \land s_3''(x_0) = s_3''(x_n),
\]

the \emph{hermitian spline}

\[
    s_3'(x_0) = u s_3' (x_n) = v,
\]

given both \(u,v\).

\subsubsection{Direct Computation}

Given our the derivatives of \(s_3\)

\begin{align*}
    s_3(x) &= a_i + b_ix + + c_i x^2 + d_i x^3 \\ 
    s_3'(x) &= b_i + 2c_i x + 3d_i x^2 \\ 
    s_3''(x) &= 2c_i + 6d_i x ,
\end{align*}

the interpolation and continuity properties are fulfilled if 

\begin{align*} 
    a_i + b_ix_i + + c_i x_{i}^2 + d_i x_{i}^3 &= y_i \\  
    a_i + b_ix_{i + 1} + + c_i x_{i+1}^2 + d_i x_{i+1}^3 &= y_{i + 1},
\end{align*}

also the continuity of the first derivative delivers the following property 

\[
    b_{i - 1} + 2c_{i - 1}x_i + 3d_{i _ 1}x_i^2 = b_i + 2c_i x_i + 3d_i x_i^2 
\]

and the second derivative the following 

\[
    2c_{i - 1} + 6_{i - 1}x_i = 2c_i + 6d_i x_i
\]

for \(i = 0, \dots, n - 1\). This provides us with 

\begin{align*}
    2_c0 + 6d_0 x_0 &= 0 \\ 
    2c_{n - 1} + 6_{n - 1}x_i &= 0
\end{align*}

This will deliver a system of equations of \(4n \times 4n\).

\textbf{Example:}

For four variables we would get the following cubic spines \(S_i\) with the main conditions:

\begin{align*}
    S_1(x_1) &= y_1 \\   
    S_1(x_2) &= y_2 \\ 
    S_2(x_2) &= y_2 \\  
    S_2(x_3) &= y_3 \\  
    S_3(x_3) &= y_3 \\  
    S_3(x_4) &= y_4 \\  
    S_4(x_4) &= y_4 
\end{align*}

the first derivative conditions for the intersection points

\begin{align*}
    S_1'(x_2) &= S_2'(x_2) \\ 
    S_2'(x_3) &= S_3'(x_3)
\end{align*}

and lastly the second derivative conditions

\begin{align*}
    S_1''(x_2) = S_2''(x_2) \\ 
    S_2''(x_3) = S_2''(x_3)
\end{align*}

\subsubsection{Computation Over Moments}

We define a \emph{moment} as 

\[
    \beta_i = s_3''(x_i), \quad i = 0, \dots, n.
\]

We also define  \(\alpha_i := s_3'(x_i)\). 

Given that our first and second derivatives are both defined, we can define our second derivate of the form 

\[
    s_3''(x) = \beta_i + \frac{x - x_i}{x_{i + 1} - x_i}(\beta_{i + 1} - \beta_i) = \frac{\beta_{i + 1} - \beta_{i}}{h_i}(x - x_i)
\]

with \(h_i = x_{i + 1} - x_i\).

If we integrate over the region \([x_i, x]\)

\[
    s_3'(x) = \int_{x_i}^{x} \frac{\beta_{i + 1} - \beta_{i}}{h_i}(x - x_i) = \beta_i (x - x_i) + \frac{b_{i + 1} - \beta_i}{2h_i} (x - x_i)^2 
\]

Giving the following line

\[
    s_3'(x) = \alpha_i + \beta_i (x - x_i) + \frac{b_{i + 1} - \beta_i}{2h_i} (x - x_i)^2 
\]

Then by repeating the integration over the same integral delivers 

\[
    s_3(x) = y_i + \alpha_i (x - x_i) + \frac{\beta_i}{2} (x - x_i)^3 + \frac{\beta_{i + 1} - \beta_i}{6h_i}(x - x_i)^3
\]

By substituting \(x = x_{i + 1}\) in \(s_3'(x)\) we get: 

\begin{align*}
    \alpha_{i + 1} &= s_3'(x_{i + 1}) \\ 
    \alpha_{i + 1} &= \alpha_i + \beta_i (x_{i + 1} - x_i) + \frac{b_{i + 1} - \beta_i}{2h_i} (x_{i + 1} - x_i)^2 \\
    \alpha_{i + 1} - \alpha_i &=  \frac{b_i + \beta_{i + 1}}{2}
\end{align*}

Continuing, by substituting \(x = x_{i + 1}\) into \(s_3(x_i)\) we get 

\begin{align*}
    s_3(x_{i + 1}) = y_{i +1} &= y_i + \alpha_i (x_{i + 1} - x_i) + \frac{\beta_i}{2} (x_{i + 1} - x_i)^3 + \frac{\beta_{i + 1} - \beta_i}{6h_i}(x_{i + 1} - x_i)^3\\
    &= u_i + \alpha_i h_i + \frac{\beta_i}{2} h_i^2 + \frac{\beta_{i + 1} - \beta_{i}}{6} h_i^3 \\
    \frac{y_{i + 1} - y_i}{h_i} &= \alpha_i + \frac{\beta_i}{3} h_i + \frac{\beta_{i + 1}}{6} h_i
\end{align*}

By evaluating on ton the inner points of an interval \([x_i, x_{i + 1}],\) \([x_{i - 1}, x_i]\) we get 

\begin{align*}
    \frac{y_{i + 1} - y_i}{h_i} &= \alpha_i + \frac{\beta_i}{3} h_i + \frac{\beta_{i + 1}}{6} h_i \\ 
    \frac{y_{i} - y_{i - 1}}{h_{i - 1}} &= \alpha_{i - 1} + \frac{\beta_{i - 1}}{3} h_{i - 1} + \frac{\beta_{i}}{6} h_{i - 1}
\end{align*}

Now by subtracting the two equations we get

\begin{align*}
     \frac{y_{i + 1} - y_i}{h_i} - \frac{y_{i} - y_{i - 1}}{h_{i - 1}} &= \alpha_i - \alpha_{i - 1} + \frac{\beta_i}{3} h_i + \frac{\beta_{i + 1}}{6} h_i - \frac{\beta_{i - 1}}{3} h_{i - 1} - \frac{\beta_{i}}{6} h_{i - 1} \\ 
     &= \frac{\beta_i + \beta_{i + 1}}{2} h_{i - 1} + \frac{\beta_i}{3}h_i + \frac{\beta_{i + 1}}{6}h_i - \frac{\beta_{i  -1}}{3}h_{i - 1} - \frac{\beta_i}{6}h_{i - 1} \\
     &= \frac{h_{i - 1}}{6}\beta_{i -1} + \frac{h_{i - 1} + h_i}{3}\beta_i + \frac{h_i}{6} \beta_{i + 1}
\end{align*}

more specifically we get the following system of equation 

\[
    h_{i - 1}\beta_{i -1} + 2(h_{i - 1} + h_i)\beta_i + h_i \beta_{i + 1} = 6\left(\frac{y_{i + 1} - y_i}{h_i} - \frac{y_{i} - y_{i - 1}}{h_{i - 1}}\right)
\]

for \(i = 1, \dots, n - 1\). This is a tridiagonal system of equations which can be solved in \(O(n)\) time.

\[
    \begin{bmatrix}
        2(h_0 + h_1) & h_1 & 0 & \dots & 0 \\ 
        h_1 & 2(h_1 + h_2) & h_2 & \dots & 0 \\ 
        0 & h_2 & 2(h_2 + h_3) & \dots & 0 \\ 
        \vdots & \vdots & \vdots &  & \vdots \\ 
        0 & 0 & 0 & \dots & 2(h_{n - 2} + h_{n - 1})
    \end{bmatrix}
    \begin{bmatrix}
        \beta_1 \\ 
        \beta_2 \\ 
        \beta_3 \\ 
        \vdots \\ 
        \beta_{n - 1} 
    \end{bmatrix}
    = 
    \begin{bmatrix}
        \gamma_1 \\
        \gamma_2 \\
        \gamma_3 \\
        \vdots \\
        \gamma_{n - 1}
    \end{bmatrix}
\]

where

\[
    \gamma_i = 6\left(\frac{y_{i + 1} - y_i}{h_i} - \frac{y_{i} - y_{i - 1}}{h_{i - 1}}\right), \quad h_i = x_{i + 1} - x_i
\]

Every thing but the \(\alpha_i\) is know, but they can be computed by

\[
    \alpha_i = \frac{y_{i + 1} - y_i}{h_i} - \frac{\beta_i}{3} h_i - \frac{\beta_{i + 1}}{6} h_i
\]

Hence, we get the following algorithm for computing the cubic spline interpolation. 

\subsection{Algorithm For Cubic Spline Interpolation}

Given the points \((x_0, y_0), \dots, (x_n, y_n)\), we can compute the cubic spline interpolation by following steps:

\begin{enumerate}

    \item Compute \(h_i\) and \(\gamma_i\) for \(i = 1, \dots, n - 1\).

    \item Solve the tridiagonal system of equations \(A\beta = \gamma\).

    \item Compute all \(\alpha_i\)

    \item Substitute the values of \(\alpha_i\) and \(\beta_i\) into the formula for \(s_3(x)\) to get the cubic spline interpolation.

\end{enumerate}

\section{Interpolation Curves}

We define a continuous function 

\[
    \gamma: [a, b] \subseteq \R  \to \R^m, \quad t \to \gamma(t)
\]

as \emph{curve} with a parameter \(t \in [a, b]\).

Now given \((t_i, z_i), t_i \in [a, b], z_i \in \R^m\) we want to find \(f:[a, b] \to \R^m\) such that 
\(f(t_i) = z_i\ \forall i\). We will use \emph{polynomial curves}

\[
    f(t) = 
    \begin{bmatrix}
        p_1(t) \\ 
        \vdots \\ 
        p_m(t)
    \end{bmatrix}
\]

where \(p_j(t)\) is a polynomial of degree \(n\).

One of the methods for computing such polynomials are Newtonian Divided Differences but instead of using individual 
\(y_i\) we use the vectors with the corresponding values from \(x_i\) for the differences.
